Overview & Rationale

Why bowel technique matters — SSI data, the scope of the problem, and the evidence supporting routine use.

🎯
Purpose of Bowel Technique: To prevent cross-contamination of the surgical abdominal wound by peritoneal microorganisms, thereby reducing the risk of a surgical site infection (SSI). Applies to all GI procedures — both open and endoscopic — whenever the gastrointestinal tract is entered.

Evidence Supporting Bowel Technique

Instrument Culture Study
Contamination Is Consistent & Significant
A focused study cultured needle holders and tissue forceps used for anastomoses of the small intestine and colon. Results showed consistent contamination for both, but a significantly higher contamination level for instruments used during colon surgery — underscoring the need for strict bowel technique.
Literature Review & Clinical Audit
Bowel Technique Is Effective
A second study found that the use of bowel technique is effective in preventing SSIs. The combined results support the practice of bowel technique on all surgical procedures involving entry into the GI tract — open and endoscopic.

Key Definitions at a Glance

SSI Classification
Surgical Site Infection
Infection occurring within 30 days of a surgical procedure. Classified as: superficial incisional, deep incisional, or organ/space. SSIs are the 3rd most frequently reported nosocomial infection.
Bowel Technique
Also Called…
Bowel isolation technique · Intraoperative bowel technique · Isolation technique · Clean and dirty scrub technique · Clean closure technique. All refer to the same essential practice of isolating contaminated instruments and supplies from clean ones during GI procedures.
When It Applies
All GI Entries
Bowel technique begins when the GI tract is opened and ends once the tract is closed. Applies to all procedures involving entry into the gastrointestinal tract — both open and endoscopic procedures.
Team Responsibility: All members of the surgical team should be involved in the process of developing and implementing surgery department policies and procedures for the consistent use of bowel technique.

Guideline I — Bowel Technique Principles

The core intraoperative protocol: when technique begins and ends, two Mayo stand setup, sterile field management, gown/glove changes, and clean closure.

⚠️
Guideline I: The principles of bowel technique should be utilized by the surgical team in order to avoid cross-contamination and reduce the risk of SSI to the patient.

Intraoperative Protocol — Step by Step

Step A — GI Tract Opens
Verbal Confirmation & Contamination Begins
▼
  • The sterile surgical team must verbally confirm that the GI tract is being opened/entered
  • All items that contact an open GI tract — or are handled with gloved hands that have contacted it — are considered contaminated. This includes: instruments, sponges, suction tip, Asepto®, light handles, Bovie tip, gloves, and gowns
  • The CST should ensure the contaminated Mayo is isolated from the clean Mayo and back table immediately
Step B–C — During GI Entry
Maintaining Separation of Clean and Contaminated Fields
▼
  • Contaminated Mayo must remain isolated from the clean Mayo and sterile back table at all times
  • The CST and all sterile team members must not directly handle supplies and instruments on the clean Mayo or back table until the GI tract is closed and gowns/gloves are changed
Accessing the Back Table: If the CST needs an additional instrument or supply from the back table during the contaminated phase, place a sponge stick inside a small basin (kidney or round) and use it to retrieve the item. Touch only the ring-handles of the sponge stick and return it to the basin for possible further use.
Step D — Wound Draping
Sterile Towels & Wound Protector Around Incision
▼
  • Sterile towels should be placed around the surgical incision site prior to the GI tract being opened to aid in reducing cross-contamination and preventing SSI from GI tract spillage
  • According to surgeon's preference, a polyethylene wound protector may also be used
Step E — GI Tract Closes
Clearing the Contaminated Items Before Gown/Glove Change
▼

Once GI tract closure is complete, the CST performs the following in sequence:

1
Remove contaminated items from the incision area
Remove Asepto®, suction tip, Bovie tip, and light handles and place with contaminated instruments on the contaminated Mayo. Remove sterile towels or polyethylene wound protector from around the incision.
2
Discard contaminated sponges
Throw contaminated sponges into the sponge bucket.
3
Move the contaminated Mayo
Move the contaminated Mayo away from the sterile field but within sight of the CST so instrument and sharps counts can be completed with the circulating person.
4
Hand off the specimen basin
The contaminated basin containing the surgical specimen is handed to the circulator.
Step F — Gown & Glove Change
Entire Sterile Team Changes — CST First
▼
🥼
Order Matters: After closure of the GI tract and removal of contaminated items, the entire sterile surgical team changes gowns and gloves. The CST changes first, then assists the other sterile team members in donning new sterile gowns and gloves.
  • Change occurs after removal of contaminated items and after GI tract closure
  • CST changes his/her gown and gloves first
  • CST then assists other sterile team members in donning new sterile gowns and gloves
Step G — Clean Closure Setup
Restoring the Sterile Field After Gown/Glove Change
▼

After changing gowns and gloves, the CST completes the following to prepare for clean closure:

  • New sterile laparotomy drape placed over the contaminated drape, or incision site squared off with new towels placed over the contaminated drape (per surgeon's preference)
  • New suction tip and new Bovie tip placed
  • Clean sponges brought onto the sterile field
  • New Asepto® obtained
  • New light handles positioned
  • Clean Mayo positioned for clean closure
Clean Closure Technique: The use of clean instruments (needle holders, forceps, skin stapler, etc.) that were isolated from contaminated instruments during the GI procedure — used to close the layers of the surgical wound.

Guidelines II & III

Annual P&P review requirements and continuing education standards for CSTs in bowel technique.

Guideline II
Annual Review of Bowel Technique Policies & Procedures
▼
  • The surgery department must review all bowel technique P&Ps on an annual basis
  • Review must include a multidisciplinary group: CSTs, surgeons, RNs, risk management, and the infection control officer
  • The department must document: when P&Ps were reviewed, any revisions made, and who participated
  • All CSTs must be familiar with current P&Ps for bowel technique
  • New employee orientation must include reviewing the bowel technique P&Ps
Standardized Routine: The surgery department should establish a standardized routine for the use of bowel technique that all CSTs follow for every GI procedure. A consistent, standardized routine used by all CSTs is essential to reducing SSIs.
Guideline III
Continuing Education for CSTs on Bowel Technique
▼
🎓
CE on bowel technique is essential to preventing SSIs and emphasizing the principles of asepsis and sterile technique. CE must be based on andragogy (adult learning theory — Malcolm Knowles): relevant, practical, and learner-active.

Recommended Instructional Methods

Lecture-Based Methods
Engage the Learner
  • Case study presentations for discussion
  • Audience discussion with suggestions for improving and reinforcing bowel technique
Other Proven Methods
Varied Instruction
  • Interactive training videos
  • Computerized training modules
  • Teleconferences
  • Simulated practice of bowel technique prior to implementing in a surgical procedure
  • Education in multiple languages if necessary
Delivery Format: CE should be delivered in short modules over time — not in a single lengthy session. Programs must be periodically evaluated for effectiveness, including feedback from surgery department personnel.

CE Records — 3-Year Minimum Retention

Required Documentation
What Must Be Recorded
  • Dates of each continuing education session
  • Names and job titles of employees who completed CE
  • Synopsis of each CE session provided
  • Names, credentials, and experience of instructors
Retention Period
Minimum 3 Years
All education records must be maintained for a minimum of three years by the surgery department. This is a regulatory and accreditation requirement.

Formal competency requirements and measurable criteria for Certified Surgical Technologists in bowel technique and aseptic principles.

Key Facts to Memorize

When It Starts
GI Tract Is Opened
Bowel technique begins the moment the GI tract is opened/entered. The sterile team verbally confirms this event.
When It Ends
GI Tract Is Closed
Bowel technique ends once the GI tract is closed. Only then can gowns and gloves be changed and clean closure proceed.
Who Changes First
The CST
The CST is always the first person to change gown and gloves after GI closure, then assists all other sterile team members.
Back Table Access
Sponge Stick in Basin
Use a sponge stick inside a small basin (kidney or round) to retrieve items from the back table. Touch only the ring-handles. Never directly handle the clean back table during the contaminated phase.
Contaminated Mayo
Isolated but In Sight
After GI closure, the contaminated Mayo is moved away from the sterile field but must remain within sight of the CST so instrument and sharps counts can be completed.
Wound Protection
Before Opening — Not After
Sterile towels (and optional polyethylene wound protector) must be placed before the GI tract is opened — not after spillage has already occurred.

Key Terms & Glossary

Definitions as established in the AST Guidelines for Best Practices in Bowel Technique (Approved 2008, Revised 2017).

Asepsis
Absence of bacteria, viruses, and other microbes. The fundamental goal of sterile technique in the perioperative setting.
Bowel Technique
Techniques used during gastrointestinal procedures to isolate contaminated instruments and supplies from the clean ones, to prevent the patient from acquiring a postoperative surgical site infection. Also called: bowel isolation technique, intraoperative bowel technique, isolation technique, clean and dirty scrub technique.
Clean Closure Technique
Use of clean instruments — e.g., needle holders, forceps, skin stapler — that were isolated from contaminated instruments used during a GI procedure, to close the layers of the surgical wound after gown and glove change.
Clean and Dirty Scrub Technique
See Bowel Technique. An older term for the same practice of maintaining two separate instrument setups — one clean, one contaminated — during GI procedures.
Cross-Contamination
The unintentional transfer of bacteria and/or other microbes from healthcare personnel to the patient, or when the patient or healthcare personnel come into contact with a contaminated fomite. Bowel technique specifically aims to prevent this.
Healthcare-Associated Infection (HAI)
Infections that patients acquire during the course of receiving treatment for other conditions within a healthcare setting. SSIs are the most common HAI, accounting for 31% of all HAIs in hospitalized patients.
Principles of Asepsis
Practices applied by surgical team members to prevent the patient from acquiring a postoperative surgical site infection. The basis of all sterile technique in the OR.
Sterile Technique
The techniques used to put the principles of asepsis into practice. Includes proper gowning, gloving, draping, instrument handling, and bowel technique during GI procedures.
Surgical Site Infection (SSI)
An infection that occurs within 30 days of a surgical procedure. Classified as: superficial incisional, deep incisional, or organ/space. SSIs are the 3rd most frequently reported nosocomial infection, with a 3% mortality rate and a cost of $3.2 billion per year.
Andragogy
The theory of adult learning developed by Malcolm Knowles. Adults learn best when information is relevant to their work experience, practical rather than academic, and when the learner is actively involved. Required basis for CST continuing education per Guideline III.
Polyethylene Wound Protector
A disposable device placed at the surgical incision prior to opening the GI tract to further reduce the risk of wound contamination from GI spillage. Used per surgeon's preference in addition to sterile towels.
HDO (Healthcare Delivery Organization)
Any healthcare facility or organization responsible for developing, approving, and implementing surgery department policies and procedures — including those for bowel technique — per their own protocols.
